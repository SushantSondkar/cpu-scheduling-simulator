    🎯 Problem Statement 55: Core Objective & Insights ▼

    Problem Statement 55: "Compare average turnaround times of all algorithms on a single process set and highlight the best."

    In multiprogramming systems, the CPU scheduler decides which ready process receives the CPU core. A primary benchmark of scheduling quality is the Turnaround Time (TAT): the total elapsed time between process submission and its final completion.

    Turnaround Time (TAT) = Completion Time (CT) - Arrival Time (AT)
    Average Turnaround Time = (Σ TAT) / Number of Processes (N)

    Key Takeaway: Shortest Remaining Time First (SRTF) is provably optimal for minimizing average turnaround and waiting times when all burst times and arrival times are known in advance.

    📐 Essential Scheduling Formulas & Definitions ▼
    1. Completion Time (CT): The timestamp at which a process finishes its execution.
    2. Turnaround Time (TAT) = CT - AT
    3. Waiting Time (WT) = TAT - BT (time spent waiting in the ready queue)
    4. Response Time (RT) = First CPU Allocation Time - AT
    5. CPU Utilization = (Total Busy Time / Total Simulation Makespan) × 100%
    6. Throughput = Number of Completed Processes / Total Makespan
    Strict Context Switch Convention

    A context switch represents CPU overhead saving and restoring process control blocks (PCBs). In accordance with academic operating system principles:

    • Context switches are counted only when the CPU transitions from one real process to a different real process.
    • Transitions at the very start ($t=0$) or at the very end are not counted.
    • Idle periods do not represent process state saves and are not counted as context switches.
    ⚖️ Tie-Breaking Rules & Queue Conventions ▼
    1. First-Come, First-Served (FCFS)

    Orders arrived processes by earliest arrival time (AT ASC). If arrival times are identical, ties are broken by lower PID (PID ASC, e.g. P1 before P2).

    2. Shortest Job First (SJF - Non-Preemptive)

    Among all arrived processes, picks the one with the smallest burst time (BT ASC). Ties are broken by earlier arrival time, then lower PID. Once running, it cannot be preempted.

    3. Shortest Remaining Time First (SRTF - Preemptive)

    At every discrete time unit, picks the candidate with the smallest remaining burst. Crucial Rule: If an arrived candidate has remaining time equal to the currently running process, the CPU does not preempt. Other ties are broken by earlier arrival then lower PID.

    4. Priority Scheduling (Non-Preemptive & Preemptive)

    User toggle determines whether lower numerical values denote higher priority (default ON). Preemptive priority preempts only when an arrived process has strictly higher priority than the running job. Equal priority does not trigger preemption.

    5. Round Robin (RR) Simultaneous Arrival Rule

    When a running process exhausts its time slice at the exact same instant $t$ that a new process arrives, the new arrival is enqueued first into the ready queue, followed immediately by the re-enqueued preempted process.

    🎓 Viva Voce Frequently Asked Questions & Answers ▼
    Q1: Why does SRTF generally produce the lowest average turnaround time?

    A: SRTF is a greedy algorithm that prioritizes processes requiring the least amount of remaining work. By finishing short jobs earlier, their turnaround times are drastically minimized, and they leave the ready queue sooner, dramatically reducing the cumulative waiting time experienced by all other processes.

    Q2: What is the Convoy Effect in FCFS?

    A: The Convoy Effect occurs when a CPU-bound process with a huge burst time arrives first, forcing numerous short I/O-bound or interactive processes to sit idle in the ready queue, drastically inflating average waiting and turnaround times.

    Q3: Why is Round Robin preferred in time-sharing systems despite having higher average turnaround time?

    A: Round Robin optimizes for Response Time and fairness rather than turnaround time. Every process receives guaranteed CPU attention within $(N - 1) \times q$ time units, ensuring interactive responsiveness for end users.

    Q4: What is the impact of choosing an extremely small or extremely large Round Robin quantum?

    A: If quantum $q$ is extremely small, CPU spends an excessive percentage of cycles on context switching overhead rather than productive execution. If $q$ is extremely large ($q \ge \max(BT)$), Round Robin degenerates into FCFS.

    Q5: Can Priority Scheduling cause Starvation, and how is it resolved?

    A: Yes, low-priority processes can starve indefinitely if higher-priority processes keep arriving. The standard operating system solution is Aging, gradually increasing the priority of waiting processes as time passes.
